I'll start by saying *I probably have too many basses*.

I don't really *collect* basses, in the sense that many collectors accumulate instruments for value, appreciation.
not really to *play* them.

Every bass I have has a story, a reason I bought it.  Some I picked up new, some used.
I consider all of them *working* basses - they are meant to be played, not sit in a case, or hanging on a wall.

## What I don't have

So far I'm missing uprights and short-scales.
I have an itch for both, but not quite ready for either.

I don't have vintage instruments either, other than a beat up '78 P/J quietly waiting for a restore.

## Categories

Before jumping into the inventory,
it is important to understand the categories and characteristcs my basses fall into:

### Gigging vs Recording

I have basses I like to perform with, vs. what I might record (or loan to someone to record).

The needs can be different:

- Performances favor one bass (perhaps with a backup) that can suitably cover all the songs I might play
- Recording can be highly specific (if looking for a specific sound)

My Fenders arrived to my collection mostly to support my home studio recording options for others - I was working towards sound engineering and music production versus being a serious bass player.  There is a long history for that, but not subject for discussion now.

The Precision and Jazz Basses are passive, and give an iconic sound.
Music production favors them because it is typically easy to get them to sit in a mix,
while other basses might require some hunting to get classic sounds out of them.

Similarly, I chose to pick up Sterlings for similar reasons - they have iconic late 70's and early 80's bass tones.

Getting back into playing seriously, I noticed a more natural gravitation to Ibanez - I had a history with them back in the early 90's,
and really like the Sound Gear lines for comfortable and "fast" necks for my small hands.

### Playability

I look for basses that support easy setup according to what I play:

Neck profile is conducive to:
- Technique
- Small hands
- Style of music I'm playing

Pretty vague, eh?

I find I can play most necks, with the caveat the setups are good.  There are benefits and tradeoffs of each.

Regarding action setup:
- Low on fretless - Jazz, pop/rock requiring a fretless tone - great care to articulation
- Medium-to-medium-low - my goto for most classics and rock that I play fingerstyle, across most of my basses
- Medium-to-medium-high - My precision and sterling 4 string classic basses set up for more aggressive rock and/or slap/pop.

### Comfort and position

I need to be able to play an instrument for hours if needed, with small risk of injury:

- Weight distribution: With an optimal strap, does the bass position neutrally with zero neck dive?  Does the weight distribute evenly across both shoulders?
- Neck/scale characteristics: I can comfortably reach the entire scale length
- Body Contour: Sits against my body without cutting into it.  I can rest my plucking/picking/slapping arm on top of the body comfortably.
- Weight: Ideally less than 10 lbs, although can relax this if the above characteristics are optimal

### Reliability/Durability/Maintenance

What I look for here is consistency:

- I can set up the bass and largely leave it untouched - it plays predictably every time.
- The intonation is consistent - The only part of the setup I have to change is simply tuning, mostly that I can predict based from temperature or humidity.
- If any part of the setup is active, how often do I need to swap batteries, is it predictable?  Or can I goto passive mode on the fly if necessary?
- Minimal noise
- Pots, electrics are mostly carefree

Every bass in my collection is suitable for recording or gigs, although I notice Fenders tend to be inconsistent out-of-box (may require pro setup, even for mid-tier).

Ibanez and Sterlings have been very consistent out-of-box, and easy to tweak the setup.

I've had some inconsistent battery drain, and, I'm getting intermittent pickup noise out of my Ibanez SRMS725.  It hosts the Fishman Fluence active setup that has no passive backup mode - I got this one used, and it has gotten some tough love during a couple of gigs, so I'm not ready to critize Ibanez.

I have the same setup on my Ibanez EHBMS Fishman Fluent bass, and no problems with it, although the battery drain is faster for like playing than the Sterlings.

All that said, I am shying away from active-only basses for gigs - I've been burned a couple of times with what I would have qualified as a bad batch of batteries.

#### Number of Strings

I typically favor 5 string basses, especially for jams/gigs - they cover almost all the songs I would play, and give the flexibility to transpose (play songs in different keys) without having to re-tune the bass).

I love the simplicity of 4 string basses - and respect that is *constrains* the player.  For many classics I just love the feel of a Precision or Jazz style 4 string bass.

It's not really about the number of strings - They are distinctly different instruments.

#### Frets

- Number of frets - this is a bit of misnomer - it's not really about the frets themselves, but the number of diatonic notes the player gets on a neck configuration regardless of whether the instrument is fretted, or not.  Standard-scale classic Fender basses typically have 20.  Ernie Ball Music Man basses typically have 21 or 22.  Modern basses may have 24.  But it is important the neck and body ergonomically support playing all those notes to be worthwhile.

- Fret materials - All my basses have medium size frets - the compromise between playability (fretting), action setup (medium-ish) and wear is good.  Some of my basses have nickel-silver or stainless steel.  Nickel-silver wears faster, but easier for DYI touchups than stainless steel.

- Fret markers - My night vision (low contrast) is not so great, I need visible markers for cues if I'm making large positional changes.  I switch up between classic, modern basses with different scale lengths, so building muscle memory is a problem.  I have not found great options for fret markers - the "glow-in-the-dark" inlays on my Ibanez's are very weak.  So I like the large block inlays the best.

- Fretless - this flips the arguments about frets.  I don't play much, so need for fret line markers is critical.  I have to give the same diligence for intonation as a fretted bass.

### Active vs Passive

The electronics characteristics can include passive and/or active elements:

Active elements using externally (battery) powered pickups and/or EQ/controls.
Passive do not.

It is a common fallacy to classify a bass as either active or passive.
It can include a combination of the following:

- Passive pickups - Single, Split, or Humbucker style copper wound pickups
- Passive volume/tone controls - works with passive pickups
- Active onboard EQ - working with either passive or active pickups
- Fully active configuration with active pickups and EQ - EMG and Fishman Fluents are examples  

Classic basses typically include passive pickups, volume and tone controls.

Modern basses (including updates of classic models) can include a combination of passive pickups with an option to run passive volume/tone controls, or passive pickups with an active onboard EQ.  Or, the pickups and EQ setup may be fully active.

I tend to avoid fully active configurations during jams and gigs, and favor basses that allow a minimum of a fallback to passive mode if a battery fails.  Bonus points if the active and passive modes are gain-matched (the active EQ at flat levels matches the gain level of passive mode).  To be fair, some of the higher end modern basses have battery level indicators, or backups to avoid the dead battery on a gig issue.   My basses are mid level and don't have that feature.

### Fender Precision

The hypothetical question: what bass would you pick if you were stranded on an island.

This bass would be #2.

The neck profile of this bass is pretty good, with a short radius (comfortably curved).

The signal chain is really simple:

- A single set of split coil pickups.
- Tone control.

What is great about the bass:

- The opposing polarity of the pickup wiring cancels out noise.
- The positioning of the split pickups centers on the string between neck and bridge,
and gives a great punchy low-mid character tone.

### Fender Jazz

The hypothetical question: what bass would you pick if you were stranded on an island.

This bass would be it.

The neck profile of this bass is perhaps the best of any 4 string basses I've played, at least for a player with small hands:

- It is narrow, especially at the nut
- The radius is short (comfortably curved)

Both of these mmake muting more comfortable with a slightly more relaxed (curved) index finger.

The signal chain is a simple yet flexible passive configuration:

- Single coil bridge and neck passive pickups.  They are wired with opposing polarity.
- Separate volume controls for each pickup - output is aggregated (blended).
- Single Tone for the blended output.

The tradeoff with the single coil pickups is that they are susceptible to noise.

What is a cool, and usually desirable feature of the Jazz bass is what happens when boosting both pickups:

- The opposing polarity of the pickup wiring cancel out noise.
- The differing position of the pickups with respect to the string result in some mid frequencies being cancelled out, while reinforcing others.  This may give a "scooped mids" characteristic that is highly desired in genres of music like Funk, R&B, and some modern rock.  Listen to Marcus Miller for a good example.
